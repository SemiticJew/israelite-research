God Loves Black People

Semitic Jew • Article
Give

Abstract

Black people have spent centuries being taught theology through nations that enslaved them, colonized them, renamed them, and then presented themselves as the authoritative interpreters of God. The result has often been a distorted picture in which whiteness appears close to holiness while Blackness appears associated with curse, servitude, or divine rejection. Scripture teaches no such Law.

This article argues that God’s relationship with Black people must be examined through Scripture, covenant, lineage, judgment, and restoration — not through European racial theology. Within the Semitic Israelite framework presented here, we understand ourselves as Semitic Negroes: a ruddy-skinned Semitic people whose lineage is identified through Shem. What the modern world later classified broadly as “Black” should not be allowed to replace the older biblical framework through which we understand ourselves.

We are Semitic by lineage and ruddy in complexion; “Black” is a later racial designation applied to peoples whom European societies grouped together under their own systems of classification. Blackness, or ruddy-brown complexion, does not place a people outside the biblical world. Nor does captivity mean that God has ceased to love the people He disciplines.

God Did Not Create Rich Melanin as a Defect

Any serious biblical discussion about race has to begin before slavery, colonialism, America, Europe, or modern racial classifications.

It begins with creation.

Genesis declares that mankind was created in the image of God. Gen 1:26–27 does not create one class of humanity in the divine image and another class beneath it. Human dignity precedes "white" empires. It precedes nationality. It precedes the racial hierarchies constructed by later civilizations.

Therefore, ruddy-brown skin cannot logically be evidence of inferiority before God. It's quite the opposite. It's Gods' default.

Skin color is part of human physical diversity. It is not a measurement of righteousness, intelligence, divine favor, or spiritual worth.

The problem was never that ruddy-colored people lacked value before God.

The problem was that wicked men created systems in which they assigned themselves greater value.

Our ruddy complexion was never the curse. Men created the curse and then blamed God for it.

Ruddy Before “Black”

There is an important distinction in terminology.

When this article uses the word Black, it is often referring to the later historical designation imposed upon dark-skinned peoples in the modern racial world. It is not our preferred starting point for biblical identity.

Biblically, we use ruddy to describe the reddish-brown complexion associated with the ancient world and explicitly described in Scripture. David, for example, is described as ruddy in 1 Sam 16:12 and 1 Sam 17:42. The Hebrew word translated “ruddy” is admoni, a term associated with a reddish or reddish-brown appearance.

That does not mean every biblical use of the word “ruddy” identifies an entire modern racial population. Rather, it demonstrates something important: ruddy complexion belongs naturally inside the biblical world.

Our framework therefore does not begin with the European racial category “Black.”

We understand ourselves first as Semitic Negroes — a ruddy-brown Semitic people descended through Shem.

Centuries later, European racial systems increasingly grouped peoples of rich melanin under classifications such as Negro, colored, and Black. Those terms became part of our historical experience, particularly in the Americas.

So when we say Black people in this article, we are speaking in the language by which our people came to be known in the modern world.

When we speak biblically about ourselves, however, our framework is more precise:

Semitic in lineage. Negro in historical physical classification. Ruddy-brown in complexion.

“Black” came later.

Semitic Negro, Not Afro-Semitic

Modern language often speaks of “Afro-Semitic” peoples. That terminology can unintentionally carry an assumption that “Semitic” represents one category of people while “African” or Black identity must be attached to it from the outside.

That is not the framework used here.

We use the term Semitic Negro.

The order matters.

Semitic describes lineage and belonging. Negro describes physical identity.

In the biblical framework advanced by Semitic Jew, our identity is traced through Shem. We therefore do not understand ruddy-brown skin as something foreign to Semitic identity.

We understand ourselves as a ruddy-skinned Semitic people.

The popular imagination has been trained to hear “Semitic” and picture pale Europeans or modern Middle Eastern populations. But Scripture never defines Shem by European racial categories.

Those categories were developed thousands of years after the biblical genealogies they are now used to interpret.

The Bible should interpret our understanding of the biblical world—not nineteenth- and twentieth-century racial assumptions.

Covenant Is Evidence of Concern

One of the strongest demonstrations of biblical love is covenant.

God’s relationship with our ancestors the Israelites was never merely sentimental.

He chose.

He instructed.

He protected.

He judged.

He corrected.

He restored.

Deuteronomy repeatedly describes Israel as a people uniquely bound to God through covenant. Deut 7:6 describes Israel as a people set apart unto the LORD. Amos 3:2 makes an equally important point: special relationship brings special accountability.

God tells Israel, in substance, that because He knew them uniquely, He would punish their iniquities.

That is a crucial distinction.

Judgment is not the same thing as hatred.

A father may discipline a son precisely because the son belongs to him.

Heb 12:6 expresses the principle clearly: whom the Lord loves, He chastens.

Therefore, national suffering cannot automatically be interpreted as evidence that God hates the suffering people.

Within the covenant framework, punishment may demonstrate the exact opposite: that the covenant remains morally significant.

Captivity Does Not Mean Abandonment

Israel’s biblical history contains repeated periods of oppression, captivity, displacement, and foreign domination.

Egypt.

Assyria.

Babylon.

Foreign occupation.

Scattering.

Yet captivity never erased the language of restoration.

Deut 30:1–5 anticipates Israel being scattered among nations and then gathered again.

Jeremiah speaks of restoration after judgment.

Ezekiel describes a scattered people whom God remembers and gathers.

Isaiah repeatedly combines punishment with promises of redemption.

The pattern is consistent:

Sin. Judgment. Captivity. Repentance. Restoration.

That pattern matters when discussing the history of the people later called Black.

A people may lose land.

They may lose national names.

They may lose language.

They may be sold.

They may be scattered.

They may be renamed by their captors.

None of those things logically prove that God has forgotten them.

Scripture contains precisely the opposite pattern: God repeatedly remembers people whom history appears to have erased.

God Hears the Cry of the Oppressed

Exodus is one of the clearest demonstrations of God’s concern for an oppressed people.

The Israelites were economically exploited.

Their labor was controlled.

Their population was feared.

Their children were targeted.

Their suffering became institutional rather than accidental.

Then Scripture says God heard their groaning and remembered His covenant. Ex 2:23–25.

Notice what the text does not say.

It does not say oppression proved that God had abandoned Israel.

Their oppression was real.

Their suffering was real.

Their captivity was real.

And their covenant was still real.

Pharaoh’s power did not cancel God’s promise.

That principle should matter deeply to the people later called Black whose history contains enslavement, forced labor, segregation, racial violence, and systematic humiliation.

Human beings can place chains on a people.

They cannot place chains on God’s covenant.

Christianity Often Taught Black People the Opposite

The tragedy is that Christianity in the Western world frequently presented itself as the religion of the Bible while participating in systems that degraded Black people.

Slaveholders quoted Scripture.

Segregationists attended churches.

Colonial governments sent missionaries alongside imperial administrators.

Images of God, Christ, angels, prophets, and apostles were steadily Europeanized.

Eventually a psychological association developed:

White became sacred.

Black became fallen.

That association did not come from Scripture.

It came from power.

European civilizations did not merely conquer land. They often conquered imagination.

Once a people can be convinced that God looks like their oppressor, biblical interpretation itself becomes psychologically colonized.

The question then changes from:

What does Scripture say?

to:

What did our captors teach us Scripture says?

Those are not the same question.

Black People Are Not a Biblical Afterthought

The people later called Black are often taught to approach Scripture as outsiders looking into someone else’s history.

That assumption deserves examination.

The biblical world stretches across regions populated by peoples who would never fit later European concepts of whiteness. Egypt, Cush, Canaan, Arabia, Mesopotamia, and the broader ancient Near East formed an interconnected world of Semitic, Hamitic, and neighboring peoples.

Scripture was not written in medieval Europe.

Abraham did not walk through England.

Moses did not confront a Scandinavian Pharaoh.

David did not reign in France.

The prophets did not preach in Germany.

The biblical narrative developed in an ancient Near Eastern and northeastern African environment long before modern racial categories existed.

Therefore, the assumption that the biblical world was fundamentally European—and that ruddy, richly melanated people must somehow be inserted into it later—is historically backward.

The ancient world came first.

Europe’s racial reinterpretation came later.

God’s Love Does Not Mean God Approves Everything We Do

There is another error that must be avoided.

To say that God loves the people later called Black does not mean that those people are incapable of sin.

Scripture does not teach racial innocence.

Israel sinned.

Judah sinned.

Kings sinned.

Priests sinned.

Prophets confronted their own people repeatedly.

Biblical love is not indulgence.

God’s love calls people to repentance, obedience, justice, discipline, and truth.

If we claim covenant while rejecting covenant responsibility, we have misunderstood covenant.

Israel was never chosen merely to feel special.

Israel was chosen to obey.

Deut 10:12–13 connects relationship with duty: fear God, walk in His ways, love Him, serve Him, and keep His commandments.

Love therefore produces responsibility.

The Messiah Does Not Teach Black Self-Hatred

Nothing about the teaching of Christ requires the people later called Black to despise themselves, erase their lineage, or adopt European cultural identity in order to approach God.

Christ teaches repentance.

Faithfulness.

Justice.

Mercy.

Obedience.

Love of God.

Love of neighbor.

Truth.

The New Testament does not command any people to become culturally European.

Nor does salvation require the abandonment of national history.

Acts 17:26 acknowledges nations and their appointed boundaries. Revelation continues to speak of peoples, nations, kindreds, and tongues.

Biblical unity is not the destruction of human distinction.

It is righteous order beneath the authority of God.

Chastening Is Not Hatred

Perhaps the most important point is this:

The suffering of the people later called Black must not automatically be interpreted as proof of divine hatred.

That argument would collapse under the weight of Scripture itself.

Joseph suffered.

Job suffered.

Jeremiah suffered.

Israel suffered.

Judah suffered.

The apostles suffered.

The Messiah suffered.

Suffering by itself cannot tell us whether God loves someone.

The proper question is what God has said.

And throughout Scripture, God repeatedly declares His willingness to hear repentance, remember covenant, judge oppressors, restore the broken, and gather the scattered.

The biblical God is not indifferent to oppression.

Ps 103:6 declares that the LORD executes righteousness and judgment for the oppressed.

Isa 10 condemns unjust decrees.

Jer 22 condemns those who exploit labor without justice.

James condemns wages withheld from workers.

God repeatedly positions Himself against oppression.

God Did Not Forget Us

For generations, Black people have been told competing stories.

One story says we are cursed.

Another says we have no meaningful biblical identity.

Another says our history begins with slavery.

Another says civilization came to us from Europe.

Another says God belongs to everybody except the people who suffered while carrying His book in chains.

Scripture gives us reason to challenge those stories.

Our history does not begin on a plantation.

Our relationship to the biblical world should not be interpreted through the racial imagination of Europe.

And the suffering of the people later called Black does not prove divine abandonment.

If we understand ourselves as a Semitic Negro people descending from Shem, then the task before us is not to manufacture an identity.

It is to examine Scripture, history, genealogy, language, migration, captivity, and covenant carefully enough to recover what was obscured.

But even before every historical question is resolved, one biblical truth remains clear:

The people later called Black are human beings created in the image of God.

Oppression does not erase that image.

Slavery does not erase that image.

Colonialism does not erase that image.

Mockery does not erase that image.

And no racial theology developed by men has the authority to reverse what God established at creation.

Conclusion

God loves Black people — the ruddy people whom later societies came to classify under the modern racial designation “Black.”

Not because modern society finally decided Black lives have value.

Not because a government passed a civil-rights law.

Not because a church issued an apology.

And not because European theology granted Black people admission into the biblical story.

Our value begins with God.

The God of Scripture created mankind in His image, entered covenant with a people, heard the cries of the oppressed, judged powerful nations, disciplined those He loved, remembered the scattered, and promised restoration.

For the Semitic Negro, that produces neither racial arrogance nor racial shame.

It produces responsibility.

If we are a people of covenant, then we must become a people of obedience.

If we have been lied to, we must become lovers of truth.

If our history has been obscured, we must study.

If our fathers forgot, we must remember.

And if captivity convinced us that God hated us, Scripture gives us reason to reconsider.

Judgment is not abandonment.
Captivity is not erasure.
Our ruddy complexion is not a curse.
And God has not forgotten His people.
