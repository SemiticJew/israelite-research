Who Defined Man First? Adam, Human Origins, and the Error of European Racial Authority

Semitic Jew • Article
Give

Abstract

Before European scientists measured skulls, constructed racial charts, divided humanity according to complexion, and developed theories of human hierarchy, Scripture had already answered the more fundamental question of what a human being is. Genesis begins human origins with Adam, a man formed by God from the earth, given life by the breath of the Creator, and made in the image of God.1

This article argues that later European racial systems should not be permitted to function as authorities over a biblical text that existed long before those theories were created. Within the Scripture-first interpretation advanced here, Adam was an earth-toned, ruddy-brown man whom modern racial language would identify as Black. The term “Black” is modern; Adam was not. The physical point is that Scripture gives no reason to begin human origins with a pale European image.

Human Origins Begin With Adam

Genesis does not begin human history with a continent, empire, university, racial category, or scientific institution. It begins with God. Genesis 1:26 records the Creator saying, “Let us make man in our image, after our likeness,” and the following verse states plainly that God created man in His own image. Genesis 2:7 then narrows the focus further by identifying the first man: “And the LORD God formed man of the dust of the ground, and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life; and man became a living soul.”1

The biblical sequence matters. Adam did not receive humanity from a government, an anthropologist, or a racial theorist. God formed him, God gave him life, and God gave him identity and purpose. Before there were Egyptians, Israelites, Greeks, Romans, Englishmen, Germans, or Americans, there was Adam. Human nations therefore come after humanity; humanity does not originate from the later political or racial categories created by nations.

The relationship between Adam and the ground is also significant. The Hebrew name Adam is closely related to adamah, the word used for ground or earth. The biblical narrative begins with an earth-formed man, not with European geography or European appearance. Within the interpretive framework presented here, that language is consistent with a ruddy, earth-toned original man rather than the pale figure later normalized through European religious art.

When we say that Adam was Black, we are using a modern racial word to describe physical appearance, not claiming that Adam walked through Eden identifying himself according to modern American racial categories. The category “Black” came much later. The argument is simpler: the first man should not automatically be imagined as a pale European merely because later European artists painted him that way.

Human origins do not begin with Europe. They begin with Adam, and Adam begins with God.

God Defined Man Before the Nations Did

The most important biblical statement concerning human identity is not about skin color. It is that man was made in the image of God. Genesis 1:27 declares that God created man in His own image, and Genesis 9:6 later grounds the value of human life in that same fact.2 Human dignity therefore precedes modern racial classification, colonial rule, citizenship, wealth, education, and national power.

This is why later racial hierarchies must be judged against Scripture rather than allowed to judge Scripture. Human institutions may classify, rename, or reorganize populations, but none of those institutions created man. If God established human identity first, then every later theory enters the discussion as a secondary claim rather than as the final authority.

This is particularly important because racial systems have often depended upon dehumanization. Before a population can be enslaved, colonized, experimented upon, dispossessed, or killed without public outrage, it is useful for those in power to describe that population as naturally inferior, primitive, savage, or somehow less human. Scripture disrupts that logic by placing human worth in the act of creation itself. A racial hierarchy cannot remove an image that God placed upon mankind.

European Nations Were Not the Prophets of God

European nations produced scholars, explorers, scientists, theologians, universities, governments, and institutions. Some of their work contributed significantly to human knowledge. But none of that makes European nations the prophets of God in the earth. Moses was a prophet. Isaiah was a prophet. Jeremiah was a prophet. European racial theorists were not.

That distinction is important because modern readers frequently approach the Bible through assumptions that were developed thousands of years after the biblical text was written. A European anthropologist may propose a racial theory, a European painter may depict a biblical figure according to his own appearance, and a European institution may create terminology for African peoples, but those actions do not carry prophetic authority. Their claims must be examined like the claims of every other nation.

Scripture should not be forced to submit to categories that did not exist when the text was written. If a later racial system conflicts with the biblical genealogy, physical description, or historical environment of the text, the later system should be questioned rather than treated as unquestionable truth.

The Hamitic Hypothesis and the Manufacture of Racial Authority

The so-called Hamitic hypothesis is an important example of how European racial theory was imposed upon Africa and then attached to biblical language. In the nineteenth century, John Hanning Speke described peoples of East Africa through racial assumptions that treated supposedly “Hamitic” populations as civilizing outsiders.3 Charles G. Seligman later systematized similar ideas in The Races of Africa, first published in 1930.4

The basic pattern treated advanced African societies as difficult to explain if the people living in those societies were regarded as Black Africans. Civilization, political organization, agriculture, architecture, and social complexity were therefore attributed to supposedly superior outside populations. These theories were later rejected, but their history shows how racial assumptions can be dressed in the language of scholarship.

Genesis itself does not teach this hierarchy. Genesis 10:6 simply records, “And the sons of Ham; Cush, and Mizraim, and Phut, and Canaan.”5 The text gives genealogy. It does not teach that Ham produced a superior Caucasian race sent into Africa to civilize supposedly inferior Black populations. That racial structure was imposed upon the biblical names later.

The difference between the text and the later theory matters. Moses gives genealogy; racial anthropology added hierarchy. The biblical record remained while the racial theory changed.

Adam, David, and the Meaning of Ruddy

European visual tradition also influenced the way many readers imagine biblical people. Once European paintings became culturally dominant, the faces in the artwork were often treated as though they were the faces described by Scripture. Yet a painting is not genealogy, and an artist’s imagination is not a verse.

This is especially important when Scripture uses the word “ruddy.” First Samuel 16:12 describes David as “ruddy,” and First Samuel 17:42 uses the description again.6 Nothing in either passage says that David was pale, white, or European. The word describes reddish coloration. It does not establish a modern racial category.

Reddish coloration is fully compatible with a dark-brown complexion. A dark-brown animal can carry a deep red or warm-brown cast without becoming pale. In the same way, describing David as ruddy does not require a European complexion. The surrounding lineage remains important: David was the son of Jesse, of Judah, descended through Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham. His appearance should therefore be interpreted within that biblical lineage and historical world rather than through later European racial imagination.

The same principle applies to Adam. Genesis 2:7 forms Adam from the earth, while the name Adam itself is related to the ground from which he was formed. Scripture does not provide a modern racial label for him, but within the physical reading presented here, an earth-toned, ruddy-brown man is more consistent with the text than the pale European Adam normalized in later Western artwork.

Piltdown Man and the Fallibility of Institutions

The history of Piltdown Man provides another useful warning about human authority. In 1912, fossil remains found in England were presented as evidence of an important human ancestor. For decades the specimen influenced discussions of human origins. In 1953, detailed investigation demonstrated that the remains were fraudulent: the skull and jaw did not belong to the same creature, the teeth had been altered, and the bones had been artificially stained.7

The lesson is not that all science is fraudulent, nor that every scientific institution is dishonest. The lesson is that human institutions are fallible. Scientists can be deceived. Scholars can operate within the assumptions of their own age. Prestigious institutions can accept false conclusions and later correct them.

That reality should produce intellectual humility. It makes little sense for people who claim to believe Scripture to treat every later European theory as though it possesses greater interpretive authority than the biblical text itself. Scientific inquiry may study human anatomy, genetics, migration, and physical variation, but it does not become prophecy simply because it is scientific.

Modern Racial Labels Cannot Replace Biblical Genealogy

The same caution should be applied to broad modern terms such as “Sub-Saharan Africa.” Geographically, the phrase can describe regions located south of the Sahara. The problem arises when geography is treated as though it were a single race, culture, lineage, or historical identity. Africa contains numerous peoples, nations, languages, migrations, and genealogical traditions. Scripture itself distinguishes Cush, Mizraim, Phut, Canaan, Ludim, Anamim, Lehabim, Naphtuhim, Pathrusim, Casluhim, and other peoples rather than flattening the continent into one category.8

A line drawn across a modern map cannot create a race. Modern terminology can be useful for geography or population studies, but it should not be permitted to erase older identities or replace the genealogical distinctions preserved in Scripture.

Blackness Does Not Create the Image of God

None of this means that skin color is what made Adam human. Adam was human because God created him as man and made humanity in His image. His complexion belongs to the physical body God formed, but complexion itself is not the source of human dignity.

This distinction is necessary because correcting European racial imagery should not simply create another racial hierarchy in reverse. Scripture does not teach that darker skin makes a person more human, more righteous, or more valuable before God. It teaches that man bears the image of the Creator.

That is why the biblical argument against racial dehumanization is stronger than any argument based merely upon political equality. Human worth does not depend upon the permission of a government, the approval of an empire, or the conclusions of a scientific institution. Those authorities may recognize human dignity, but they did not create it.

Who Has the Right to Define Humanity?

The question therefore returns to authority. If God created man, then God possesses the first authority to tell humanity what it is. Genesis establishes man as a created being, formed from the earth, given life by God, made male and female, placed in the image of God, and entrusted with purpose and dominion.9

Later nations created racial classifications, scientific theories, maps, political systems, and cultural images. Some of those tools were useful. Others were deeply shaped by prejudice and political power. None of them existed when Genesis was written, and none of them possess the authority to rewrite the biblical account.

For that reason, the pale European Adam should not be accepted simply because Western religious culture repeated the image for centuries. Adam belongs first to Scripture, not to Renaissance art. Human origins begin with the man God formed from the earth, and within the Scripture-first interpretation presented here, that man was ruddy, earth-toned, and physically consistent with what the modern world would call a Black man.

Conclusion

Before racial charts, before anthropology departments, before colonial boundaries, and before European theories of human hierarchy, Genesis had already defined man. Adam was formed from the earth, given life by God, and made within humanity’s original identity as the image of the Creator. That biblical order should remain the starting point for any discussion of human origins.

The argument that Adam was Black does not require us to force a modern racial category backward into Eden. It requires us to recognize that the pale European Adam is itself a later racial image. Scripture never identifies the first man as European, and the earth-toned language surrounding Adam gives no reason to make pale skin the default image of original humanity.

European nations were not the prophets of God in the earth. Their scholars and institutions may be studied, respected, criticized, or corrected like those of every other people, but their theories do not stand above Moses. The Hamitic hypothesis changed. Piltdown Man collapsed. Racial terminology continues to change. Genesis remains.

The Creator did not wait for the nations to tell Him what He made. He formed man, breathed life into him, established his identity, and declared His creation “very good.” The question, then, is not whether humanity has been defined. The question is whether we will begin with the definition given by the Creator or with nations that entered the discussion thousands of years later.
