Pharmakeia: How Nations Are Deceived

Semitic Jew • Article
Give

Preface

Growing up, I remember political commercials where white “European” Americans ridiculed one another under the respectable labels “Democrat” and “Republican.” Each side promised to fix the country. Somehow, both wanted us to believe God was on their side. The American flag supplied patriotism. Cultural appropriation of biblical language supplied moral approval. Repeated ads made the claims feel familiar and true. None of that established approval from the Most High God or Jesus Christ, His Son. Why do people keep defending the same old tired American messages, and what does pharmakeia have to do with all of this?

First, what does pharmakeia mean?

Pharmakeia is a Greek word written φαρμακεία. The King James Version translates it as “witchcraft” in Galatians 5:20 and “sorceries” in Revelation 18:23. It is not a combination of two separate words. It belongs to a Greek word family associated with drugs, potions, and sorcery. The Liddell–Scott–Jones Greek lexicon records several ancient uses, including administering drugs, poisoning, spells, and witchcraft.1 That does not mean every use of the word carries every meaning at once.

Consider the English word “spell.” A teacher asking a child to spell a word is not asking the child to perform witchcraft. The sentence tells us which meaning applies. The same care is necessary here. Thayer’s Greek lexicon identifies sorcery or magical arts in Galatians 5:20 and connects Revelation 18:23 with the deception associated with idolatry.2 The word’s history matters. But we must also read what each Israelite writer, inspired by the Holy Spirit, is saying (2 Peter 1:21).

Revelation connects sorcery with deception

Revelation 18:23 says the nations were deceived through Babylon’s sorceries. In that same verse, its merchants are described as powerful people. Elsewhere in the chapter, kings and merchants benefit from their relationship with Babylon (Revelation 18:3). Its trade includes luxury goods and human beings (Revelation 18:11–13). The chapter also holds Babylon responsible for bloodshed (Revelation 18:24). The mention of human beings among traded goods should make an American reader stop and think. American slavery involved people being bought, sold, transported, and forced to produce wealth for others. We should not discuss national prosperity while leaving those people out of the account.

Today, Black contributions to sports, science, technology, and other fields also deserve recognition. Those contributions are not automatically the same as enslaved labor. The question that carries across is who produces the value, who controls it, and who receives the reward. That context matters. Revelation places deception within the portrayal of a wealthy and destructive power. It does not present sorcery as an isolated curiosity with no relationship to the wider society.

The warning reaches beyond the familiar pictures of witches standing over pots. It concerns nations being deceived while wealth, influence, and violence exist together. This does not make pharmakeia another Greek word for advertising. It gives us a biblical reason to examine how deception operates around power. What are people being taught to trust? Who benefits from that trust? And what conduct becomes easier to excuse once that trust is established?

Who taught us what divine authority looks like?

Consider the familiar European image of Jesus: light or pale skin, straight flowing hair, and features shaped by European artistic traditions. A painting shows how an artist represented Jesus. It does not establish what Jesus actually looked like. Scripture identifies Jesus through his people and lineage. Hebrews 7:14 places him in Judah. It does not give European painters authority to supply a historical portrait. Jesus’ identity as an Israelite of Judah should not be replaced with a European racial identity or an Arab identity. His lineage matters. A modern racial label does not settle his appearance.

The concern becomes greater when an inherited image is treated as though it came with the text. A person can encounter that image in a church, a children’s book, a film, and a political message. So, the repetition of the same representation can make it seem unquestionably true. Yet seeing an image repeatedly does not establish its accuracy. A European depiction can also encourage an association between European appearance and divine authority. That deserves critical examination, especially when institutions within that same culture claim to guard biblical truth while their histories include human trafficking, greed, and exploitation.

This does not establish the motives of every artist or viewer. It asks what the image teaches and whether that lesson comes from Scripture. The Bible warns against replacing divine authority with human tradition (Mark 7:7–9). That warning applies to familiar pictures as well as familiar sermons when they are granted authority the text does not give them.

When did America become God’s chosen nation?

Religious language can make a national ambition sound like a divine assignment. A country can call itself blessed. A politician can describe its military power as righteous. A preacher can suggest that defending the nation means defending God. That is the standard I want applied when examining Pete Hegseth’s use of Scripture or John Hagee’s arguments about the modern state of Israel: show the statement, show the passage, and explain whether the conclusion follows.

Neither a government position nor a pulpit makes someone’s interpretation unquestionable. Criticism of a government’s conduct is not, by itself, opposition to God. Deuteronomy 7:6 identifies Israelites as the people chosen for a particular covenant relationship. Psalm 147:19–20 identifies Jacob and Israel as recipients of God’s word, statutes, and judgments. Neither passage names the United States. Nor does borrowing those passages establish that a modern institution possesses the same covenant position.

America cannot establish that it inherited Israel’s covenant position by borrowing biblical language. Calling a policy “Christian” does not prove it is just. Calling a war necessary does not prove God commanded it. Even Israel’s covenant relationship did not remove accountability. God connected that relationship with responsibility and judgment (Amos 3:1–2). The threatened judgment included serving enemies in hunger and hardship (Deuteronomy 28:48). It also included being offered for sale as bondmen and bondwomen (Deuteronomy 28:68). Calling the judgment “exile” must not hide the enslavement and suffering described in the text.

Now, if being chosen did not give Israelites permission to do wrong, how does an American campaign slogan give a politician permission to exploit people? Taxation and other policies must be examined by what they demand, whom they burden, and whether they are just. Scripture condemns laws that deny the poor justice (Isaiah 10:1–2). The flag is not a permission slip from heaven to “Do What Thou Wilt.”

Republican or Democrat is not a biblical verdict

Two familiar statements show how political labels can replace examination: “No Democrat can be Christian.” “If you criticize Trump, you have Trump Derangement Syndrome.” The first statement treats a party label as enough to settle a person’s faithfulness. To defend that conclusion, someone would need to explain the relevant beliefs, conduct, and biblical standard. The label alone does not do that work. The second statement dismisses criticism by attacking the critic’s judgment. Calling someone “TDS” does not answer evidence that a statement is false or an action is wrong.

Some criticism of Trump may be unfair. Some may be well supported. The accusation does not tell us which is which. Apparently, asking for evidence now requires a medical diagnosis. The same standard applies when Democrats dismiss criticism of their own leaders without addressing it. A familiar party does not deserve a different standard of truth. Scripture requires impartial judgment (Leviticus 19:15). It also warns against answering before hearing the matter (Proverbs 18:13).

If dishonesty is wrong when the other party does it, changing the campaign sign does not make it righteous. A political label can tell us someone’s affiliation. It cannot establish that their argument is true or their conduct is just.

Entertainment can carry the same message

Entertainment communicates through stories, images, sympathy, and admiration. A film can present a government as the defender of humanity. A religious production can present a particular appearance of Jesus as historical fact. A television character can make one kind of authority appear wise and another kind appear dangerous. The questions are straightforward: Who is presented as trustworthy? Whose violence is excused? Who gets to explain their actions? Whose suffering is treated as unimportant?

These questions must be applied to specific productions. They should not become accusations made without examining the work. A viewer does not need to accept a story as a history lesson for its message to deserve scrutiny. Nor does being entertained make a claim true. Good music, strong acting, and an emotional ending cannot supply missing evidence. The biblical instruction is to examine what we receive and hold to what is good (1 Thessalonians 5:21). We should be able to enjoy a story without handing it authority over our judgment.

Propaganda does not need to look like a lie

Propaganda uses selected information, repetition, and emotion to shape public opinion. It can include true details while leaving out facts that would change how people understand the story. Scripture describes people using smooth words to deceive the unsuspecting (Romans 16:18). It also warns about calling evil good and good evil (Isaiah 5:20). Neither passage names modern television. Both give us standards for examining its messages. A politician’s advertisement can show a flag, a church, and a smiling family while saying little about who benefits from the proposed policy. The images invite trust. They do not supply evidence.

The Bible even describes people speaking lies in God’s name when He had not sent them (Jeremiah 23:25–27). Adding “God bless America” to a message does not establish that God approved the message.

Millions for the coach. Restrictions for the players.

LSU announced a ten-year, $95 million contract, plus incentives, for Brian Kelly in November 2021.3 Large compensation packages are defended as the price of attracting coaching talent. But when players gain earning power, the conversation turns to restrictions, national rules, and protecting college sports. Trump’s July 24, 2025 executive order called for restrictions on certain third-party pay-for-play arrangements and federal action toward uniform rules.4 The stated distinction between legitimate endorsements and recruiting payments deserves examination. So does the unequal treatment of the people earning the money.

In other words, institutions should not celebrate enormous compensation packages for coaches while treating players’ earning power as a threat that the federal government must contain. The coach gets a fortune. The player gets a lecture about the love of the game and the importance of developing discipline. Calling one payment “market value” and another a danger to the institution does not establish a fair standard. The athletes supply the labor, face the physical risks, and help produce the entertainment being sold. Their compensation should not become suspicious simply because they have gained leverage to negotiate at the table.

Scripture recognizes a worker’s claim to compensation (1 Timothy 5:18). It condemns withholding compensation from workers after their work is done (James 5:4). It also condemns unequal measures (Proverbs 20:10). This connects directly to the argument about deception: language such as “protecting the game” can make restrictions on workers sound like a public good while hiding whose interests those restrictions serve. As the sons of TMH, we must examine whose income is protected and whose bargaining power is reduced. We should also examine how public money is used, rather than assume every coaching dollar comes directly from taxes.

If the market justifies millions for the coach, then players deserve an explanation for why their opportunity to earn just as much requires special restraint when they are the ones doing the work on the field.

Follow the money without skipping the evidence

Revelation’s merchants matter because the chapter places wealth alongside the deception of nations. Acts gives another example of religious belief and financial interest working together. Demetrius earned money through silver shrines associated with Diana, also called Artemis. He warned fellow craftsmen that Paul’s teaching threatened their trade (Acts 19:24–27). His defense involved both religious honor and income. That is a concrete example of a financial interest helping to motivate the defense of a religious practice. It gives us reason to ask similar questions today.

Who receives donations when a political conflict is presented as a holy battle? Who sells access, merchandise, or entertainment around the message? Who loses influence if people stop believing it? Receiving money does not, by itself, prove that a claim is false. It identifies an interest worth examining. Jesus warned against serving both God and mammon (Matthew 6:24). Money cannot become the authority that decides which truths may be spoken and which leaders must be protected.

Does this make everything pharmakeia?

No. An inaccurate painting, a dishonest advertisement, and an actual practice of sorcery are not automatically the same thing. Israel’s law names prohibited practices involving divination, enchantment, and consulting spirits (Deuteronomy 18:10–12). We should identify what someone is doing before applying that accusation. The argument here is that Revelation connects sorcery with the deception of nations within a wealthy and violent order. That warning invites us to examine modern systems of influence. It does not remove the need to prove each modern claim.

The same principle applies to medicine. The Greek word’s association with drugs does not establish that every medicine is sorcery. A shared word history cannot erase differences in purpose and conduct. If our argument against deception depends on an exaggerated definition, we have created another problem instead of exposing the first one.

God did not sign the campaign ad

Revelation’s warning joins the deception of nations with powerful merchants. That should trouble anyone who watches religious language used to protect political loyalty and financial interests (Revelation 18:23). When a politician’s conduct is answered with “God chose him,” the claim of divine approval is doing work the evidence has not done. When a coach’s millions are celebrated but a player’s earning power becomes a national problem, “protecting the game” needs a much better explanation.

These messages ask ordinary people to defend someone else’s authority, income, or reputation as though God’s honor depends on it. It does not. The Most High does not need us to excuse dishonesty to protect His name. Job asks whether people will speak wickedly and deceitfully on God’s behalf (Job 13:7). That question belongs in the pulpit, the campaign office, and the television studio. If your defense of a leader requires you to excuse what God condemns, you are defending the leader — not obeying God.
