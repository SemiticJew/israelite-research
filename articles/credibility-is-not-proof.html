Credibility Is Not Proof

Semitic Jew • Article
Give

By Semitic Jew | Companion to “Was Adam the First Man?” — Season 3, Episode 10

In this episode, I ask teachers to show where Scripture places a population before Adam or assigns his creation to an eighth day. A respected teacher cannot make those details appear by speaking confidently. Genesis connects Adam with humanity’s creation (Genesis 5:1–2). Paul calls him the first man (1 Corinthians 15:45). Anyone proposing another reading must explain those passages.

That demand for evidence also belongs outside the pulpit. European and American institutions have promoted fraudulent discoveries, racial hierarchies, and unsupported theories about human ability. Their history gives us concrete reasons to examine their claims rather than surrender our judgment to their reputation.

Piltdown: a discovery manufactured to fit a story

In 1912, Charles Dawson and Arthur Smith Woodward presented remains from England as evidence of an early human ancestor. Piltdown Man eventually proved to be a forgery: human skull fragments combined with an orangutan jaw, with staining and altered teeth helping sell the deception. The fraud was exposed in 1953.1

The fossil had an impressive introduction. It still was not genuine. Scientific testing helped uncover the fraud, which is precisely why reputation must never replace examination. Piltdown demonstrates a failure in evaluating supposed evolutionary evidence; exposing one forgery does not, by itself, disprove evolution. The sound argument is narrower and stronger: a discovery must survive testing even when influential people want it to be true (Proverbs 18:17).

When prejudice wore a scientific title

German physician Franz Joseph Gall developed phrenology, which claimed that the outside of the skull could reveal mental qualities. Its followers turned heads into maps of character and ability. Related racial-ranking practices used bodily measurements to lend authority to claims of European superiority.23 A tape measure can record a distance. It cannot turn prejudice into a discovery.

English statistician Francis Galton coined “eugenics” in 1883. Its advocates treated complex human qualities as though they followed simple hereditary rules and promoted control over who should reproduce. Those beliefs helped support forced sterilization and exclusion.3 These were influential programs, not merely somebody’s strange opinion in a basement. Institutional approval gave harmful claims power; it did not make them sound.

Scripture locates humanity’s creation in God’s image (Genesis 1:27). It does not assign human worth by skull shape. And shared ancestry does not erase the families and nations recorded in Scripture (Genesis 10:32). Neither point requires accepting racial hierarchies invented by later institutions.

A child is more than a percentile

Modern pediatric head measurements need to be distinguished from phrenology. Tracking an infant’s head circumference can help identify unusual growth and conditions such as hydrocephalus. That is a medical use of measurement, not a reading of personality or racial superiority.4 Using the same body part does not make two methods the same practice.

Height and weight percentiles are not inherently false either. They locate a measurement within an age- and sex-specific distribution. The problem begins when a comparison becomes a verdict. A child at the 25th percentile for height is not “25 percent healthy,” and the 50th percentile is not a grade every child must earn.5 Babies are not failing algebra because they are short.

The CDC itself states that growth charts should not serve as the sole basis for diagnosis.6 Growth over time, symptoms, family background, and the reference chart matter. A number can be accurate while the conclusion attached to it is unsupported. That is the statistical error worth confronting.

Make the claim carry its own weight

My argument about Adam begins with the biblical text. These historical examples do not establish Adam’s chronology; they expose the weakness of treating prestigious institutions as beyond examination. A theory is not true because Europeans proposed it, and it is not false merely because they did. Its evidence must bear the weight of its conclusion.

Before accepting a claim, identify what was actually observed and what someone added. Scripture warns against answering before hearing (Proverbs 18:13). That standard applies to the professor, the physician, the preacher, and me. If a claim requires us to stop examining it in order to respect its author, the claim needs better evidence.
